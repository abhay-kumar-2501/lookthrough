How much of your retirement fund is riding on AI's data centers?

Pick the fund in your 401(k) or IRA. Lookthrough reads what it reported holding and shows how much of your money is in the six companies building AI's data centers and the companies selling them equipment, then what those six have promised to pay.

Each square is one cent of every dollar.

Both columns look at the fund's stocks only, with a company's share classes added together. "Ten largest companies" is their share of the whole fund. "Counts like" is how many equal-sized companies would give the same concentration. Cash, collateral for lent shares and bond funds are left out.

What counts, and where this comes from
  • Source. Funds must report every holding to the US securities regulator. The tool reads those reports. They are published a few months late, so the date is shown and today's mix will differ, especially after large price moves.
  • Tickers. The tool reads the holdings of nine funds. Typing a ticker matches it to one of them: the same fund, another fund tracking the same index, or the nearest kind where the note says how yours differs. The list of tickers is mine and covers common US funds, not all of them.
  • The six builders are Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Oracle and CoreWeave, among the largest buyers of AI chips and data center space. NVIDIA does not name its customers, so this is my list. Each company is counted whole, although each also has large businesses unrelated to AI.
  • The companies selling the equipment are NVIDIA plus my own list of AI hardware makers: . Each of these is also counted whole. The list is a judgment. It leaves out Intel, Samsung, the storage makers (Western Digital, Seagate, Sandisk) and the makers of chip-manufacturing equipment (Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA). Counting those too would add about 4.6 cents per dollar in an S&P 500 fund.
  • Sellers and builders are shown apart on purpose. If spending slowed, the sellers would lose sales while the builders would keep more of their cash, so the two groups do not carry the same risk.
  • Not advice. The tool shows what is in a fund. It does not say what anyone should hold.

What those six have promised to pay

The six have borrowed . Count their leases and they have promised .

The gap is rent. is for buildings and equipment they already use. is for buildings, mostly data centers, that are not ready yet: signed, and not on any balance sheet yet. Each company states that figure in one sentence of a footnote. Lookthrough collects the sentence from every report and puts it in a ledger, one bill per company.

The six: Microsoft, Alphabet (Google), Amazon, Meta, Oracle and CoreWeave. OpenAI and Anthropic are not on this list. They are private companies and do not publish financial statements, and they rent much of their computing from companies that do.

In household terms: a mortgage, a car lease you are already paying, and a lease you have signed on an apartment that is still being built.

The same table in finance terms

Debt is borrowings at carrying value (Amazon's includes its financing obligations and short-term borrowings). Lease liabilities are operating and finance leases on the balance sheet. Leases not yet commenced are discounted at the coupon on each company's most recent note of about ten years, or at its lease-book rate where that is higher (CoreWeave). The range runs from the longest stated term at the coupon on its recent note of about twenty years, to the shortest term at its liability-weighted lease-book rate. EBITDA is operating profit plus depreciation and amortization of property, equipment and intangibles over the last twelve months; Amazon's broader cash-flow add-back, which also covers leased assets and video content, is not used, and its and Alphabet's amortization of intangibles (about $1bn each) is partly estimated because neither gives it every quarter. Net of cash leaves out holdings a company may not yet sell. Earnings are held at today's level, so the pro forma ratio is a stress reading: the load if the new data centers earned nothing. For a company growing as fast as CoreWeave, the last twelve months understate what it earns now, which makes its ratio look worse. Cash is not deducted in the main columns. EBITDA here is after operating-lease rent; a rating agency would add that rent back (EBITDAR) when it counts leases as debt, which would lower every ratio in this table. That adjustment is not made here. Workings →

      Twelve months of cash: where it came from and where it went

      From each company's cash-flow statement, latest twelve months (Oracle's end on 31 August, the others' on 30 September). A blank means the statement has no such line, so the totals of those columns are minimums. Debt is the long-term lines only: short-term borrowing drawn and repaid within the year, which is large at Amazon, is left out. Alphabet reports one line for all the debt it raises, and its repayments include its finance-lease payments, which the others report separately. "New shares sold" includes shares bought by employees through company plans at Microsoft and Oracle. Buildings and equipment is cash paid, before any proceeds from sales or incentives.
