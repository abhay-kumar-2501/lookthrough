Everything else each has signed for the years ahead

"Other commitments" are purchase and construction commitments, and for some companies they are larger than the leases. They are not part of the argument because their timing is not disclosed in a comparable way. They run for up to 30 years, and they are never added across companies: one company's commitment is often another's order book.

Finding 2

Leases signed but not yet on the balance sheet, mostly for data centers

A lease is recorded only when the landlord hands over the building. Until then a 15-year data center lease appears as one sentence in the lease footnote. This chart collects that sentence from every quarterly and annual report since 2022.

Total future payments on leases that have not started, by calendar quarter. Oracle's fiscal quarters end one month earlier. These are undiscounted payments over terms of up to 30 years. Amazon's figure also covers warehouses and aircraft; Meta's and CoreWeave's include some energy, network and office leases.

What those promises are worth today

A dollar of rent due in 2040 is worth less than a dollar today. Discounting the payments at each company's own lease rate turns the headline into a figure that can sit next to debt. The method was first run on leases already on the books, where the answer is known:

Pale bar: total future payments. Solid bar: value today, middle estimate. Line: low to high (longer leases at higher rates, to shorter leases at the disclosed rate). Start dates and lease terms come from each filing; where a filing gives a wide range the middle term is 15 years.

Where this could be wrong. The discount rates are the ones each company reports on its existing leases, most of which were signed when borrowing was cheaper, so the true value is more likely below the middle estimate than above it. The back-test confirms the discounting arithmetic; it cannot confirm the start dates and terms, which is why the range is wide.

Finding 3

Who can carry it

Debt against cash earnings is the first ratio a lender looks at: how many years of today's earnings it would take to repay what is owed. The table gives it twice, on what each company owes now and again with the signed leases counted as if they had started.

TodayWith signed leases countedAbove 4×

How to read it. Cash earnings are operating profit plus depreciation over the last twelve months (EBITDA). They are held at today's level, so the second column is the load if the new data centers earned nothing: a stress reading, not a forecast. Three things make the reading harsh, most of all for Oracle and CoreWeave. Reported profit is already after rent on operating leases, while those leases are also counted as debt. Earnings are the last twelve months, which understates a company growing as fast as CoreWeave. And neither company's signed customer contracts are counted: the table shows them so the two sides can be read together. The columns after cash deduct cash and investments; a negative number means the company holds more cash than it owes.

If the equipment does not last as long as the accounts assume

A separate question from the findings above. Since 2020 every large cloud company has lengthened the useful life of its servers, which spreads the cost over more years and lifts reported profit. That changes reported earnings, not the cash measures the findings use. It matters to the argument in one way: equipment that wears out sooner has to be bought again sooner.
